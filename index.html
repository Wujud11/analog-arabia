NASA's go-to analogs are Atacama, Devon Island and Hawaii. The Arabian Peninsula has young basalt fields, a 1.5 km lava tube, a young iron-meteorite crater field in the Empty Quarter, and a dike intrusion that InSAR recorded in 2009.     The model and its assumptions

    Em = Hm × ηm × 0.85 × (1 − soiling), with Hm the monthly mean daily all-sky irradiance on a horizontal surface (NASA POWER ALLSKY_SFC_SW_DWN, kWh/m²/day).

    Cell temperature uses the NOCT model at midday irradiance: Tcell = Tmax + (NOCT − 20) × G ÷ 800, with NOCT = 45 °C and G = 1,000 W/m² (clear-sky midday), which adds about 31 °C. Tmax is the monthly mean daily maximum air temperature (NASA POWER T2M_MAX). Applying the midday cell temperature to the whole day's energy overstates the heat loss, since mornings and evenings are cooler.

    Efficiency falls with heat: ηm = η25 × (1 − 0.0035 × (Tcell − 25)), the typical −0.35 %/°C of crystalline silicon. A balance-of-system derate of 0.85 covers wiring, inverter and mismatch losses; heat and dust are modelled separately.

    Array area = daily demand ÷ minm(Em). Panels are modelled flat, which is conservative: tilted panels collect more. Night storage and batteries are not sized, and sizing for the worst month assumes no energy is stored between months.

    For context: Mars receives about 43% of Earth's sunlight at the top of its atmosphere (about 590 vs 1,361 W/m²), so Martian arrays are larger still. Arabian sites add a problem Mars does not have, heat; Mars shares the dust.

    Methodology: weighted multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA)

    The fit score is a weighted-sum MCDA. Each site is scored 0 to 5 on ten criteria, each mission is a set of weights, and Score = Σ(weight × criterion) ÷ Σ(weight × 5) × 100.

    Measured from NASA data (live), 2 of 10 criteria: thermal cycling = 5 × (mean daily T2M_MAX − T2M_MIN) ÷ 20 °C, capped at 5, because hardware on the Moon and Mars must survive large day–night swings. The Mars-like climate criterion is computed for every site from the NASA POWER climatology API (free, no key) as 0.7 × dryness + 0.3 × sunlight, where dryness = 5 × (1 − annual rain ÷ 250 mm) and sunlight = 5 × (annual solar radiation − 4) ÷ 3.5 kWh/m²/day, both clipped to 0–5. NASA POWER also supplies surface skin temperature, air temperature and wind on every site card.

    From the scientific literature: the other eight criteria (marked “rated” on each card) are rated from the sources cited on each site card. They are expert judgements and are labelled that way.

    Next measured criteria (not yet in the score): terrain roughness from SRTM/NASADEM and surface minerals from EMIT, which need Earthdata downloads we could not process in the hackathon window.

    Moon and Mars side: every Earth site is paired with a named target on the Moon or Mars, with its coordinates, the NASA mission that studied it, and a tile from NASA Moon Trek (LRO WAC mosaic) or Mars Trek (Viking colour mosaic). No Arabian site reproduces the lunar south pole's permanent cold and shadow. They match lunar lava tubes and mare basalt instead, which are candidate places for a sheltered base.

    Robustness: two Monte Carlo tests run on every view: weights varied ±50%, and every expert rating shifted by −1, 0 or +1, each over 2,000 seeded runs.

    Limitations: no Arabian site reproduces the lunar south pole's permanent cold traps; eight criteria are literature ratings, not field measurements; NASA POWER climatology is a coarse grid (about 0.5°), so it describes the region around a site, not the exact spot.

    Why this matters

    Analog testing is expensive and concentrated in a handful of famous sites. A ranked, evidence-linked shortlist lets agencies and universities in the region find a site that matches the test, not the reputation.

    Who uses it

    Rover and instrument teams choosing test terrain. Researchers planning habitat trials in lava tubes. Students and teachers who want to see Mars from the Hejaz.

    What comes next

    Automate the ratings from NASA data, add the other harrats (there are 15), and let field teams log test results back to each site.
