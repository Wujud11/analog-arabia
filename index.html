NASA's go-to analogs are Atacama, Devon Island and Hawaii. The Arabian Peninsula has young basalt fields, a 1.5 km lava tube, a young iron-meteorite crater field in the Empty Quarter, and a dike intrusion that InSAR recorded in 2009.     Methodology: weighted multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA)

    The fit score is a weighted-sum MCDA. Each site is scored 0 to 5 on nine criteria, each mission is a set of weights, and Score = Σ(weight × criterion) ÷ Σ(weight × 5) × 100.

    Measured from NASA data (live): the Mars-like climate criterion is computed for every site from the NASA POWER climatology API (free, no key) as 0.7 × dryness + 0.3 × sunlight, where dryness = 5 × (1 − annual rain ÷ 250 mm) and sunlight = 5 × (annual solar radiation − 4) ÷ 3.5 kWh/m²/day, both clipped to 0–5. NASA POWER also supplies surface skin temperature, air temperature and wind on every site card.

    From the scientific literature: the other eight criteria are rated from the sources cited on each site card. They are expert judgements and are labelled that way.

    Next measured criteria (not yet in the score): terrain roughness from SRTM/NASADEM and surface minerals from EMIT, which need Earthdata downloads we could not process in the hackathon window.

    Moon and Mars side: every Earth site is paired with a named target on the Moon or Mars, with its coordinates, the NASA mission that studied it, and a tile from NASA Moon Trek (LRO WAC mosaic) or Mars Trek (Viking colour mosaic). No Arabian site reproduces the lunar south pole's permanent cold and shadow. They match lunar lava tubes and mare basalt instead, which are candidate places for a sheltered base.

    Coordinates come from the Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program, published papers and NASA pages. Each card shows what its coordinate marks.

    Why this matters

    Analog testing is expensive and concentrated in a handful of famous sites. A ranked, evidence-linked shortlist lets agencies and universities in the region find a site that matches the test, not the reputation.

    Who uses it

    Rover and instrument teams choosing test terrain. Researchers planning habitat trials in lava tubes. Students and teachers who want to see Mars from the Hejaz.

    What comes next

    Automate the ratings from NASA data, add the other harrats (there are 15), and let field teams log test results back to each site.
